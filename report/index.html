The Great Switch: Europe’s car industry and the electric transition

Europe invented the car industry, and the industry still employs 2.4 million people in EU factories. Now the product is changing from combustion to battery, and the competition is changing from Europe to China. This report follows the market, the factories and the trade flows, then compares petrol and electric cars on what matters to a driver: cost per kilometre, and CO₂ over the whole life of the car.

01 · The market

Diesel collapsed, hybrids took over, and electric reached one car in six

Europe’s new-car market is still smaller than in 2019. Within it, the mix changed fast: diesel’s share halved in four years, and full hybrids became the most common new powertrain. Battery-electric cars kept growing after a pause in 2024, when Germany ended its purchase subsidy.

      02 · The industry

      Fewer cars built, and whole regional economies exposed

      Car output peaked in 2017 and never recovered from the pandemic and the chip shortage. Italy’s production has almost halved since 2017. Exposure is highest in Central Europe: in Slovakia and Romania, one in seven manufacturing jobs is in the car industry.

        03 · The China reversal

        From a €22 billion surplus to a deficit in eight years

        In 2017 Europe sold China €22bn of cars and bought almost none. By the flows had crossed. The EU’s anti-subsidy duties on Chinese battery-electric cars, definitive since October 2024, cut BEV imports. Imports of plug-in and full hybrids, which the duties do not cover, rose at the same time.

            04 · Petrol vs electric: cost

            Charged at home, electric is cheaper to run in every EU country

            Running cost per 100 km uses this week’s pump prices from the EU Oil Bulletin and household electricity prices from Eurostat, for a compact car (petrol 6.5 L/100 km, electric 18 kWh/100 km including charging losses). The picture changes for drivers who rely on public fast chargers: at a typical €0.60/kWh the EV costs about €10.80 per 100 km, close to petrol in the cheapest-fuel countries.

                05 · Petrol vs electric: climate

                The battery’s carbon debt is repaid within 26,000–65,000 km

                Building an electric car emits more CO₂ than building a petrol car, mainly because of the battery. Driving it emits far less, depending on how the country makes its electricity. Over a 200,000 km life, the electric car comes out ahead in every EU country. The advantage ranges from about 70% in Sweden and France, where power is nuclear and hydro, to about 20% in Poland, where it is coal.

                    07 · Methodology

                    How this analysis was built

                    Four sources (eleven datasets), one DuckDB model, and an explicit assumptions table that drives both the cost and the CO₂ comparison.

                    ModelDuckDB SQL · assumptions table

                    Simulate10,000-draw Monte Carlo · tornado

                    PublishSVG/JS report · GISCO map

                    Model assumptions

                    Central value and the range used in the Monte Carlo (triangular distributions). Grid intensity gets an extra ±10%.

                    Sources

                    • Eurostat: new registrations & fleet by motor energy (road_eqr_carpda, road_eqs_carpda), production index (sts_inpr_a, C29.1), jobs (sbs_ovw_act, C29), household electricity (nrg_pc_204)
                    • Comext (DS-045409): EU trade in HS 8703 by powertrain sub-heading, with China and extra-EU
                    • EU Weekly Oil Bulletin: pump prices incl. taxes, Euro-super 95 and diesel
                    • Ember: CO₂ intensity of electricity generation by country

                    Limitations

                    • Ember intensity covers direct generation emissions, not the full lifecycle of power plants. That slightly flatters electric cars, but this is outweighed by the conservative choice of today’s grid for the whole car life, since grids are decarbonising.
                    • Running cost covers energy only. Purchase price, insurance, maintenance and resale value are not in official statistics and are excluded.
                    • Public charging tariffs vary widely. €0.60/kWh is a stated scenario, not a measured average.
                    • The production index is published for only six member states plus the EU aggregate (confidentiality).
